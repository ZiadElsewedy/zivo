## PART 2 — HOW MOSTAQL PROPOSALS ACTUALLY WORK (current research)

Sourced from Mostaql's own blog and help center, current as of this research.

**How clients actually screen proposals.** Mostaql's own guidance to project owners describes a four-step funnel: (1) a fast first pass reading writing quality, price, and delivery time to cut the field to 3-5 candidates — this takes roughly 10-15 minutes total, not per proposal; (2) a look at the freelancer's bio, skills match, star ratings, and portfolio; (3) direct messages to the shortlist about background, work samples, and how you'd actually run the job (one big delivery vs. incremental check-ins, revision process); (4) a side-by-side comparison of price, timeline, quality signals, and responsiveness before deciding. The guide states plainly that clients treat past performance as the strongest predictor: "whoever could do it before is very likely to do it again."

**What makes a proposal stand out, per Mostaql's own writing guide:** open with a line that proves you understood the specific project and proposes a concrete angle, not a greeting; keep paragraphs short and let each one build on the last; use plain language even for technical points ("if you use technical terms, simplify them so the client understands easily"); cite only skills and experience that are actually relevant to *this* project, backed by a number or example, not a general skills dump; if your portfolio doesn't have a matching sample, consider producing a small custom one for that project.

**Does length matter?** There's no fixed minimum or maximum, but Mostaql's guidance is explicit that every sentence should earn its place — "express the idea in the fewest words while keeping the important details." Padding with restated obvious competence (e.g., "I'll deliver quality work") wastes the reader's attention rather than building trust.

**Common mistakes freelancers make, per Mostaql's official help content:** submitting the same templated proposal on unrelated projects; proposing on projects outside real expertise; inflated language like "choose me" or "cheapest price guaranteed"; promising unrealistic turnaround (a one-day promise on a week-scoped project reads as not having understood the project, and can get a proposal rejected outright); requesting off-platform contact in the proposal (against platform rules and a trust red flag); pricing so low it looks desperate rather than competitive.

**What makes a proposal feel genuinely written for this project, not copy-pasted:** referencing specific details from the brief in your own words (not restating the brief itself), addressing the client's actual stated constraint (budget, timeline, tech choice) rather than generic capability claims, and — if something in the brief is ambiguous — asking one sharp clarifying question that proves you read it closely rather than one that's already answered in the description.

**Competing with real experience but few reviews:** nothing in Mostaql's own guidance suggests review count alone is disqualifying — the four-step funnel above shows clients weigh writing quality and portfolio proof heavily *before* they even get to comparing ratings. The practical implication for you: the proposal text and the portfolio it links to have to do the trust-building work that a long review history would otherwise do for free. That raises the bar on both, but it isn't a dead end.

## PART 4 — YOUR PERSONALIZED PROPOSAL STRATEGY

**How to open:** the first line should prove you understood the client's actual problem or goal, stated in plain terms — never "I'm excited to apply" or a restatement of the project title. If you can name the underlying tradeoff or risk in their idea, that's a stronger opener than any greeting.

**What hooks work best for your profile:** a one-sentence version of your real differentiator ("I build the Flutter screens and the backend behind them, so you're not coordinating two people") when the project actually needs both ends; a direct reference to Mawaqeet when the project involves device features, sensors, or offline logic; your "clean code isn't the goal, the right code for the business is" framing when the client's brief shows they care about outcomes, not just a spec being followed — use it as a genuine observation about their project, not a stock line repeated everywhere.

**Ideal length:** 100-180 words for most projects. Up to ~250 only for genuinely complex, high-budget projects where technical depth is expected and welcomed. Short paragraphs, no bullet-point walls.

**What to mention:** only the tech stack relevant to *this* project; one honest, specific proof point (the most analogous portfolio piece, or the one review if it's relevant); a short plain-language plan or approach; a delivery estimate that matches the real scope.

**What to leave out:** unrelated skills (design, translation, PowerPoint) even though they're on your profile; generic passion statements; any mention of your review count (don't apologize for it or draw attention to it — let the writing carry the weight); a closing paragraph about "discussing timeline and budget further" — end on the plan or a direct question instead.

**How to talk about your experience:** lead with what you know how to do and how you think, not with tenure. "I build X the way that avoids Y" beats "I have Y years of experience." Cite the one portfolio piece that's genuinely closest to their project rather than listing all four.

**How to prove you understood the client's project:** paraphrase their actual stated problem in one sentence, in your own words, using a detail specific to their brief — then move straight into your approach. Never simply restate their project description back to them.

**How to stand out among 20-50 competing proposals:** specificity beats enthusiasm every time — a real technical observation, a sharp clarifying question (only if genuinely not already answered in the brief), or a two-line mini-plan will beat a warmer but generic proposal. Apply early where you can; Mostaql's own client-side guidance says the first screening pass happens fast and narrows the field quickly.

**What to never do:** never reuse the same opening paragraph across unrelated proposals; never claim a result or client you can't back up; never pad with corporate or AI-sounding phrases ("passionate about delivering high-quality solutions," "excited to leverage my skills"); never ignore a stated budget or timeline constraint; never ask something already answered in the project description; never share off-platform contact details in the proposal itself.
